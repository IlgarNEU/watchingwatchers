A privacy analysis of automatic content recognition on U.S. smart TVs

82% of U.S. households have a smart TV that can take snapshots of whatever is on screen and report them back. We measured where that data goes, and whether turning the feature off actually stops it.

    How ACR works

    Your TV recognizes what you watch

    Automatic content recognition (ACR) is built into most smart TVs. It identifies what's playing, whether it comes from an app, an antenna, a game console or a cable box.

    1. Capture

      The TV takes short audio or video snapshots of whatever is on screen.

    2. Send

      Those snapshots, or fingerprints made from them, are sent over the internet to the ACR provider.

    3. Match

      The provider compares them with a library of known content to work out exactly what you're watching.

    4. Monetize

      Viewing habits can be used for targeted ads and shared with or sold to other companies.

    Research questions

    What we set out to learn

    Little is publicly known about how ACR behaves in practice. Deployments are opaque, and smart TVs are hard to test automatically. We asked three questions.

    • Under what TV-viewing conditions is ACR-attributable traffic observed? Streaming apps, live TV, external inputs and more, across ten scenarios.
    • To what extent are ACR opt-outs offered and effective? Every scenario was run with ACR turned on and again with it turned off.
    • What TV-viewing information exposure is associated with ACR? We used controlled content to understand what data is shared by the TVs that use encrypted traffic. We also found unencrypted traffic and data reachable by outside parties.
    Method

    An automated living room

    We built a testbed that plays the same content on every TV without anyone at the remote, records all of its network traffic, and then works out which traffic belongs to ACR.

    1. Collect the data

      Run thousands of controlled viewing experiments on 12 TVs and capture every packet they send and receive.

    2. Identify ACR endpoints

      Use the controlled content to pick out the servers that respond to what's on screen, then compare their traffic across conditions.

    The testbed

    Twelve smart TVs cover the eight operating systems that run about 95% of U.S. smart TVs, and more than 80% of the TV brands with over 3% of U.S. households. The operating system is what we compare, so we chose TVs to cover every major OS first, adding a second TV where one OS is sold by several brands.

    All traffic to and from each TV was captured with the Mon(IoT)r software and the SPHERE research infrastructure. In total we ran 5,943 controlled experiments.

    Getting content onto the screen

    A control machine sends the same content to every TV through three routes, so each viewing scenario is identical across devices.

    Antenna

    HDMI-to-RF modulators broadcast our own custom channel to each TV's antenna input.

    HDMI

    IP-to-HDMI converters turn video streamed from the control machine into an HDMI signal, like a cable box or console.

    Screen mirroring

    Google Pixel phones and a Windows laptop running Pigeoncast and Miracast cast to each TV using its own mirroring protocol.

    Separating sound from picture

    Some ACR systems recognize content by its audio, others by its video. By switching each one off in turn on our custom antenna channel, we can tell which a TV relies on. The antenna is a natural ACR target, because what you watch over the air can't be tracked by any app.

    Running it without a remote

    We wrote control scripts using each OS's Python remote-control library for six of the operating systems. Xumo OS and VIDAA OS had no reliable library, so we drove them through their Android remote apps using ADB.

    The hard part was that TV menus change, so each OS needed its own tested sequence of commands. An IP camera on every TV recorded each run, and we reviewed the frames to confirm it worked. Runs that failed because of internet outages or unexpected TV behavior were repeated.

    How we answer each question

    • Under what viewing conditions does ACR send traffic?We identify each TV's ACR endpoints, then compare the traffic they receive in each of the ten scenarios.
    • Are ACR opt-outs offered, and do they work?Every scenario runs 30 times with ACR on and 30 times after opting out in the TV's settings, in a random order so one test can't bleed into the next. We then compare ACR traffic between the two states.
    • What viewing information is exposed?Where ACR traffic is unencrypted, we read it directly. Where it's encrypted, we infer what it carries from its timing, volume, destinations and protocol details. We also check whether content-delivery services leak viewing data outside ACR.

    Finding: opting out

    Turning ACR off doesn't always turn it off

    The advertised opt-out fully stopped ACR-attributable traffic on only half of the operating systems we tested.

    Finding: exposure

    Viewing data left in the open

    Beyond the companies that collect it, we found ACR viewing data reachable by anyone in a position to look.

    Sent without encryption

    Two providers sent viewing data over unencrypted connections, readable by anyone on the same network path, such as a shared Wi-Fi network or an internet provider.

    An open server

    We found an MQTT message broker (a server that relays messages between devices) that anyone could connect to without logging in. It was relaying ACR detection results from TVs as they happened.

    What it means

    The off switch needs to mean off

    • ACR runs on the operating systems behind about 95% of U.S. smart TVs, and it can see what you watch regardless of the source.
    • On half of those systems, opting out did not fully stop traffic tied to ACR.
    • Viewing data was exposed over unencrypted connections, and in one case through a server open to anyone.
